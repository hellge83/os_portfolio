## Story 9. Mentoring

> For me, developing people is not so much about formal training or taking a set of courses. It's more about gradually helping them become more independent by giving them real tasks and ownership.
>
> I have two good examples of this.
>
> The first one is about a woman who once applied for a Data Scientist position in my team. She was good at building models, and I liked the way she thought, but her SQL skills were not strong enough for the role at that time. So I decided not to hire her, but I gave her specific feedback. I told her that improving her SQL skills would be important for her future career and suggested some ways she could work on it.
>
> A few months later, I had an opening for an ML Engineer, and I remembered her. Her background was a much better fit for that role, so I reached out to her myself. We then worked together for several years. She joined as a Junior ML Engineer, learned MLOps-related skills while working with us, and eventually moved to another company, where she is now doing well.
>
> The second example is one of the analysts on my team who had a background in Data Science. We gradually started giving him more and more complex tasks. But for his development, technical skills were not the only important thing.
>
> He was used to working in a very closed way. He tried to figure out everything by himself, didn't talk about problems, and sometimes waited too long before saying that he had underestimated how long a task would take. Over time, I helped him become more open about his work: to talk about problems, ask for help, and discuss difficult situations and mistakes in his estimates.
>
> In the end, he became a fully independent specialist and now works successfully in another team.
>
> For me, in both cases, successful mentoring does not mean that a person becomes dependent on me. It means the opposite. I feel that I've done my job well when someone becomes independent and eventually can work successfully without my constant involvement.
