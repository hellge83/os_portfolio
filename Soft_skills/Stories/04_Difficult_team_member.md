## Story 4. A difficult team member

> I once had an analyst on my team who was a very private person and often missed deadlines. At first, I thought it might be a problem with estimating how long tasks would take or taking responsibility for the result. But when I looked into it more closely, I found a completely different reason.
>
> He could go very deep into a problem, trying to understand every possible detail and corner case. The main difficulty was that he didn't know when to stop and realize that the additional research was no longer worth the time and effort.
>
> At the same time, he almost never asked for help. The problem would become more and more complicated, the deadline would pass, and I would only find out when the task was already overdue.
>
> I realized that simply telling him to work faster wouldn't solve anything. First, I needed to build better communication and make it a normal part of how our team worked. So I started involving him more in team activities — regular calls, meetings, and discussions where people could openly talk about work problems, as well as share things that made them happy or frustrated.
>
> Over time, we came to a very simple agreement: if he saw that a task would take longer than expected, started going too deep into the problem, or wasn't sure whether it was time to stop, he would simply come and talk to me.
>
> I wanted him to understand that there was nothing wrong with saying, "I'm stuck," or admitting that something had become difficult. You don't always have to solve every problem alone. That's what the team is there for.
>
> Over time, the situation with deadlines improved significantly. For me, it was a good example that an employee's problem cannot always be solved with more control or more pressure. Sometimes, you first need to understand why a person behaves in a certain way, and only then find the right management approach.
